# Fringe projection puts 20 microns within reach

A home-built scanner can reach 0.02 mm, but only as depth noise and repeatability on coin-to-golf-ball-sized objects; nobody in the public record has shown an artifact-verified 0.02 mm *accuracy* from a DIY build. The route with the best physics is **phase-shifting fringe projection**: a small LED/DLP projector, one or two monochrome global-shutter USB3 cameras, a rigid aluminium frame and a stepper turntable, all wired to your laptop. Parts cost roughly **$450-650** by the research notes' tally, which is no cheaper than the **$720-960** commercial scanners that advertise 0.02 mm, so DIY wins on money only if you already own parts or choose photogrammetry. The cheapest route with a published near-target result is an OpenScan-style photogrammetry rig at about **€230-390**, which measured about 0.03 mm (2σ) on 20-25 mm spheres. Your laptop is the right brain for either route: it drives the projector as a second display over HDMI, pulls frames over USB3, and does all the decoding, and its GPU matters mainly for photogrammetry. The money and effort that decide whether you hit 20 microns go into the calibration target, thermal and mechanical stability, and a proper sphere-and-length verification, not into the camera or the computer. Expect 30-100 µm verified accuracy at first and 20 µm noise soon after; treat 20 µm accuracy as the stretch goal.

## "0.02 mm" is three different numbers, and vendors quote the easy one

Accuracy is closeness to the true dimension, precision is the scatter of repeated scans, and the "resolution" on spec sheets is usually just the spacing of points in the mesh, which does not make a measurement more accurate ([Artec](https://www.artec3d.com/learning-center/accuracy-precision-resolution)). Decide which one you need, because the cost differs by an order of magnitude.

The commercial market shows the gap clearly. The Revopoint MINI 2 brochure lists **single-frame precision up to 0.02 mm but accuracy up to 0.05 mm**, from a 2 MP depth camera, with a footnote that both were obtained in a controlled lab ([Revopoint brochure](https://dl.revopoint3d.com/brochure/mini2/mini2-brochure-en.pdf)). The Creality CR-Scan Otter claims "up to 0.02 mm at 60 mm" working distance and sells for about $720-960 ([MatterHackers](https://www.matterhackers.com/store/l/creality-cr-scan-otter-3d-scanner/sk/M5XL0M9L), [Walmart](https://www.walmart.com/ip/17463456402)). No independent calibrated-artifact test of either was found; the one hands-on figure in this class, a Creality Raptor measuring a part to within 0.03-0.04 mm, came from a single reviewer using a non-metrological method ([3DTechValley](https://www.3dtechvalley.com/creality-cr-scan-raptor-review/)). Certified performance starts far higher: the Artec Micro II offers 5 µm point accuracy with ISO 12836 certification at **$22,500-33,000** ([MatterHackers](https://www.matterhackers.com/store/l/artec-micro-ii-3d-scanner)).

The DIY record follows the same pattern. The most careful hobby test, the OpenScan Mini, fitted a 10.000 mm-radius sphere at 9.985 mm with surface deviation of 0.024-0.028 mm (2σ); its author retracted an earlier "0.008 mm" claim and wrote that he was "not yet confident enough to make a valid accuracy statement" ([OpenScan blog](https://blog.openscan.eu/posts/chasing-microns-openscan-mini-accuracy-test-part-2/)). The closest open structured-light project reported about 60 µm on a granite slab ([structured-light-python](https://github.com/helleb0re/structured-light-python)), and a custom research scanner cited by NIST landed at 0.032-0.050 mm ([NIST](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=927473)). A custom laser scanner checked against a CMM illustrates the trap: it repeated to 0.4 µm yet read a 1002.7 µm step as 1066.0 µm, a 63 µm bias ([euspen](https://www.euspen.eu/knowledge-base/ICE16-P1.65.pdf)). Repeatability comes cheaply, and it says nothing about accuracy until you test against an artifact.

## Fringe projection beats laser lines, but only inside a small sharp volume

Laser-line triangulation has a physical noise floor from speckle, δz = C·λ / (2π · sin u · sin θ), which the original authors put at "a couple of micrometers to more than 10 µm" for short-range sensors ([arXiv:1401.1946](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.1946), [FAU](https://cris.fau.de/publications/110424204)). Working that formula at the small apertures a hobby lens needs for depth of field gives roughly 7-20 µm per point, so a laser line starts at the edge of your budget; blue instead of red improves it only by the wavelength ratio, about 30%. Existing laser kits confirm it is the wrong path: Ciclop and FabScan cost $99-250 and deliver 0.1-0.5 mm ([3DSourced](https://www.3dsourced.com/rankings/best-diy-3d-scanner/)).

An LED projector is incoherent, so speckle largely disappears and noise falls to the photon limit. A published model measured phase noise of about 1/750 of a fringe period with a 5 MP mono camera and 9-step phase shifting, giving depth precision of roughly **5 µm at 240 mm working distance versus 70 µm at 900 mm** ([PMC10613632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10613632/)). Noise is therefore not your problem, provided the scanner works close and small. A DTU team built exactly this kind of rig from two industrial cameras and a consumer LG LED DLP projector and matched a GOM ATOS III on sphere-form error over a 300 mm field, though the commercial unit kept the edge in length accuracy ([Eiríksson et al. 2016](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XL-5-W8/7/2016/isprs-archives-XL-5-W8-7-2016.pdf)).

Two optical limits then set the size of what you can scan. First, lateral sampling: resolving 20 µm features needs about 10 µm per pixel, which shrinks the field of view. Second, high lateral resolution and depth of field are mutually exclusive in fringe projection ([arXiv:1504.04556](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.04556)); real 0.5x telecentric lenses list only ±1.9 mm to 3.1 mm of depth of field ([Edmund Optics](https://www.edmundoptics.com/p/0.5x-110mm-wd-in-line-compacttl-telecentric-lens/21811/), [Keyence](https://www.keyence.com/products/vision/vision-sys/ca-l/models/ca-lmh05/)). The table below is the research team's own arithmetic from standard diffraction and sampling relations, not a published measurement, but it is the most useful design guide in this report.

| What "0.02 mm" means to you | Camera | Single-view field | Sharp depth per view |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 µm depth noise, about 40-50 µm lateral detail | 5-12 MP | about 80-110 mm | about 5-10 mm |
| 20 µm point spacing, about 25 µm optical resolution | 12-20 MP | about 55-80 mm | about 1-2.5 mm |
| True 20 µm lateral detail with margin | 12-20 MP | about 30-55 mm | about 0.6-2.4 mm |

The first row is the sensible target. Anything thicker than the sharp slab needs multiple views or refocusing, and each merge adds error: the only turntable registration figures found were about 70 µm ([Chinese Journal of Lasers](https://m.researching.cn/articles/OJ145ba5afdbb493b4)), which alone would exceed the budget. Quote single-view and merged accuracy separately, and keep the number of views low.

Surface finish is the last physical constraint. Fringe projection "cannot, generally, measure specular surfaces" ([arXiv:1504.04556](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.04556)), so shiny, dark or translucent parts need matting spray, and the spray is not free of error: manufacturer figures are 8-15 µm for AESUB Blue and 2-6 µm for AESUB Orange ([AESUB Blue datasheet](https://www.123-3D.nl/pdf/DSN00007_TDS_EN.pdf), [AESUB comparison](https://3dverkstan.se/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/AESUB-Comparison-2024-November_web.pdf)). On a diameter that doubles, so the common blue grade can consume the whole 20 µm budget by itself; use the thin grades.

## The cheapest honest builds cost €280 to $650, about what a commercial unit does

Three builds emerge from the prices found. Totals are the research team's sums and include unsourced allowances for commodity items such as frames, drivers and spray, so verify before ordering.

| C. Upgraded structured light | Arducam 5 MP mono USB3 $165 (two for stereo); 0.5x telecentric lens €233 ([VA Imaging](https://va-imaging.com/products/telecentric-lens-0-5x-wd110-1-5-coaxial-light-c-mount)); gauge-block set $140 ([Tormach](https://tormach.com/gauge-block-set-36pcs-33181.html)); better target | about $1,000-1,300 | best chance at verified 20 µm on small parts |

Several choices inside those builds matter more than the totals. Buy **monochrome global-shutter** cameras: Polyga's DIY guidance prefers global shutter for structured light ([Polyga](https://www.polyga.com/diy-3d-scanner/technical-specifications)), and the Arducam USB3 modules are the cheapest ones found that plug straight into a laptop. The Raspberry Pi global-shutter camera is cheaper at $50-64 ([SparkFun](https://www.sparkfun.com/raspberry-pi-global-shutter-camera.html)) but needs a Pi as its host. Do not buy the $1,300-1,690 LightCrafter 4500 ([DigiKey UK](https://www.digikey.co.uk/en/products/detail/texas-instruments/DLPLCR4500EVM/4288515)); phase shifting interpolates far below a projector pixel, so fringe linearity and stability matter more than a premium engine. The DLP2000 is a bare module that needs a host or adaptor board, and whether the Arducam modules accept C-mount lenses was not verified. A used LED DLP projector or used industrial cameras are probably the best value, but no used-market prices were retrieved.

The second camera in Build C is the upgrade most worth paying for. With two calibrated cameras the projector becomes an uncalibrated light source whose parameters "are not necessary for the measurement process" ([arXiv:1404.7298](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.7298)), which removes the cheap projector's lens distortion and thermal drift from the geometry. The single-camera alternative, calibrating the projector as an inverse camera, reached about 73 µm RMS over a 150 x 250 x 200 mm volume in the literature ([Li, Karpinsky & Zhang 2014](https://engineering.purdue.edu/ZhangLab/publications/papers/2014-ao-calibration.pdf)).

## Your laptop is the whole computer: HDMI out, USB3 in

A wired laptop setup is the normal architecture, and for a static scanner the laptop is never the bottleneck. The projector plugs into HDMI and behaves as a second monitor showing fullscreen fringe patterns; the cameras stream over USB3; an Arduino on USB steps the turntable ([arXiv:2209.12101](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.12101)). Phase decoding is a per-pixel sum and arctangent that takes well under a second per view on a CPU by the researchers' estimate, so the GPU is optional for structured light. It becomes essential only for photogrammetry, where Meshroom's depth-map stage and COLMAP's dense stereo both **require an NVIDIA CUDA GPU** ([Meshroom manual](https://meshroom-manual.readthedocs.io/en/latest/faq/needs-cuda/needs-cuda.html), [COLMAP](https://colmap.github.io/install.html)).

The one real wiring subtlety is timing. Over HDMI "an uncertain number of irrelevant frames could be inserted before the expected video frame is displayed" ([Vision Systems Design](https://vision-systems.com/home/article/14035114/how-to-develop-a-low-cost-3d-scanner-using-structured-light)), and short exposures capture a DLP's time-multiplexed grey levels as posterised bands ([arXiv:1706.02698](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02698.pdf)). Hardware triggering solves this but is needed only for real-time rates; standard projectors and webcams work at lower rates ([SLStudio](https://github.com/jakobwilm/slstudio/)). The practical recipe, which is the researchers' inference and not a documented guide, is to show a pattern, wait 100-200 ms, then expose for a whole multiple of 1/60 s. The DTU rig used a slow 66.66 ms exposure for the same reason. A 40-60 pattern sequence then takes about 15-20 seconds per view.

For software, **SLStudio** is the strongest free starting point: GPLv3, commits as recent as November 2026, phase shifting with heterodyne unwrapping, fullscreen projector output and USB3 Vision cameras through Aravis ([SLStudio](https://github.com/jakobwilm/slstudio/)). Its README warns that hardware paths are not fully validated after modernisation, and it is designed for one camera plus one projector. For a stereo rig, writing your own Python pipeline is realistic: OpenCV supplies calibration and triangulation, SLutils provides N-step and multi-frequency unwrapping with optional CUDA and Python bindings ([SLutils](https://github.com/jhacsonmeza/SLutils)), and the Zuo et al. review covers the theory ([Zuo et al. 2018](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1469785)). For photogrammetry, RealityScan 2.x is free below $1 million revenue ([RealityScan](https://www.realityscan.com/en-US/license)) and Meshroom 2025.1 is the open alternative. Gaussian splatting and NeRF are not options; their geometry errors run 0.8-3 mm ([arXiv:2604.18205](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18205)).

## Calibration discipline, not hardware, decides whether you hit 20 microns

The DTU study is the best-documented recipe to copy. It calibrated with a checkerboard bonded to float glass, used only about 11 well-spread poses across ±40°, and found that a *smaller* camera model was more accurate: fixing the principal point made calibration "significantly more stable" and "only the k1 and k2 distortion coefficients are required" ([Eiríksson et al. 2016](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XL-5-W8/7/2016/isprs-archives-XL-5-W8-7-2016.pdf)). It projected 32 phase steps at the finest frequency plus two coarser sets for unwrapping. Many steps matter with a cheap projector because consumer projectors apply a nonlinear gamma that creates periodic phase error, and an N-step algorithm cancels all harmonics except orders near multiples of N ([Zuo et al. 2018](https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1469785)). Use at least 8-16 steps and pre-correct the patterns with a measured response curve.

The calibration target is the one part you cannot improvise. Target flatness and pattern errors propagate directly into the result, and reprojection error does not reveal them ([SPIE Optical Engineering](https://spiedigitallibrary.org/journals/Optical-Engineering/volume-56/issue-7/074101/Method-for-calibration-accuracy-improvement-of-projector-camera-based-structured/10.1117/1.OE.56.7.074101.pdf)). Budget €103 or more for a manufactured target; Edmund glass targets with ±0.001 mm feature accuracy cost €400-935 ([Edmund Optics](https://edmundoptics.co.uk/f/checkerboard-calibration-targets/39496/)). Stability comes next: camera-projector rigidity is the first hardware priority ([SLTK guide](https://github.com/jhdewitt/sltk/wiki/Hardware-Setup-Guide)), business-grade projectors do not compensate thermal drift ([PMC7600602](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7600602)), and one lab needed a 30-minute projector warm-up ([PMC10613632](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10613632/)). Mount everything on one aluminium plate, not 3D-printed brackets, and calibrate and scan in the same warm session.

Then prove the number with a cut-down VDI/VDE 2634 Part 2 test, as DTU did: fit a precision sphere at about 10 positions for form and size error, measure a two-sphere bar at 7 positions for length error, and scan a flat at 6 positions, discarding at most 0.3% of points. DTU's artifacts were known to 1-3 µm. The sphere-spacing test is the one that exposes scale error, and length tests are more sensitive to camera-model errors than sphere tests ([Precision Engineering 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0141635922002318)). OpenScan's precision spheres cost "more than €100" ([OpenScan blog](https://blog.openscan.eu/posts/chasing-microns-openscan-mini-accuracy-test-part-2/)); an economy gauge-block set is $140. Do not scale the scan from the same artifact you test with, which is the weakness OpenScan's author admitted.

## Conclusion

Twenty microns is a calibration project that happens to need a camera and a projector. The optics and the laptop are solved problems at hobby prices; the unsolved part, which no public DIY build has closed, is traceable accuracy, and that is bought with a good target, a stiff warm frame, a restricted camera model and a length artifact. That reframes the budget question: a DIY rig will not undercut a $720-830 commercial scanner on parts, but a fixed, rigid, small-field rig can be tuned for coin-sized parts in a way a handheld unit cannot, and it will teach you exactly where each micron goes.

The lowest-risk path is staged. Start with whatever camera and projector you can borrow and get the phase-shift pipeline running on the laptop; buy the calibration target and two reference spheres before upgrading any optics; add a second camera when the projector becomes the limiting error. Open questions the research could not settle include used-market prices, the real distortion of cheap C-mount lenses, close-focus behaviour of pico projectors, and whether turntable registration can be held to 10 µm. Your own sphere and length tests will answer the last one, and publishing them would fill a real gap.
